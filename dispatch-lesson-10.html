New Faces on the International Team
New Faces on the International Team
Story 1 (Lesson 10)

Since her department expanded, Anna had grown used to working in an international working environment. She still preferred to work in the office most days, although she sometimes chose to work from home on Fridays, like several colleagues on her team. She now worked with colleagues from different countries almost every week, and she reported to a manager based in the regional office rather than someone in her own building.

Anna had worked for the same company for many years, but the pace of change still surprised her sometimes. International calls now happened more frequently than before, and everyone on the team, not only the managers, was expected to join them. She knew she still used English, but not as much as some of her colleagues, who switched between languages without thinking twice.

"When my English is better, I will feel more confident on these calls," Anna told herself one afternoon. She also found herself wondering how things might be different right now. "If my English were better, I would probably raise these points myself instead of waiting for someone else to notice them first."

A few days later, while checking the outstanding balances report, she noticed that the regional totals didn't add up. The explanation in her colleague's email didn't fit what she remembered from their last conversation either. "If I remember correctly, we agreed on how these figures should be calculated only last week," she said during the call, and the mistake was corrected within minutes. So far, raising issues early like this had never caused her any trouble.

Before the Big Presentation
Before the Big Presentation

Before her first video call with the new regional team, Anna thought carefully about how to make a good first impression. She had heard that in that office, people say you should care about how you look on camera, so she made sure her lighting and background were tidy. She also remembered that colleagues there expect you to look smart, even for an online meeting.

The new regional director had a reputation as a direct person, someone known for communicating directly and expecting the same in return. Anna reminded herself that his directness was simply a trait, not a sign that he had a problem with her specifically, and she planned a short comment about the weather to break the ice before the meeting turned serious.

A few days later, Anna had to deliver a presentation in English to the same team, summarising the outstanding invoices for the quarter. She remembered that a strong opening helps you engage your audience from the first slide, so she began with a short, direct question instead of a long introduction. She also remembered the advice a trainer had once given her: don't focus on grammatical rules, and don't worry about your mistakes if a sentence comes out slightly wrong.

Before showing any numbers, she explained the rules regarding how outstanding invoices were classified, then gave a short update concerning a change to the invoicing process. She made sure everyone understood the purpose of the presentation before opening the floor to questions, and by the end, nobody was confused about the figures.
