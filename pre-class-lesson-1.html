Before You Read

Six Phrases to Know

think on your feet
phrase / verb
improwizować; reagować na bieżąco
To respond or make decisions quickly, without time to prepare in advance.
“In a fast-moving meeting, you need to think on your feet.”
get your point across
phrase / verb
przekazać swój pogląd; wytłumaczyć, o co chodzi
To communicate your idea or opinion clearly and successfully so that others understand it.
“It was a noisy discussion, but she managed to get her point across.”
buy yourself time
phrase / verb
zyskać na czasie
To do or say something that gives you more time to think or act before having to respond.
“He asked a clarifying question to buy himself time before answering.”
take the floor
phrase / verb
zabrać głos
To begin speaking in a meeting or public situation; to claim your turn to speak.
“She took the floor and presented the quarterly results.”
lose the thread
phrase / verb
stracić wątek
To lose track of what you were saying or following; to become confused in the middle of speaking or listening.
“He lost the thread halfway through his explanation and had to start again.”
read the room
phrase / verb
wyczuć atmosferę; odczytać nastroje w pomieszczeniu
To understand the mood, feelings, or dynamics of a group or situation, and respond accordingly.
“A good manager knows how to read the room before raising a difficult topic.”

The meeting is in English. Around the table – or on the screen – are colleagues from Sweden, Belgium, or Germany. The agenda moves fast. Someone asks for your view. You know exactly what you think. But by the time you have found the right words, the conversation has moved on.

Later, back at your desk, you replay the exchange. The thought is clear now – in Polish, obviously. But in that room, in that moment, it stayed locked away.

If this sounds familiar, you are not alone. Speaking up in a second language is a skill, not a talent. It can be learned, practised, and improved. Here is where to start.

Prepare before you walk in

The biggest mistake professionals make is waiting for inspiration to arrive during the meeting. In a second language, thinking on your feet is genuinely harder – your brain is doing two jobs at once. The solution is simple: remove the surprise element.

Before your next meeting, write down two or three points you want to raise. Not a script – just a direction. When the moment comes, you will not be searching for ideas and words simultaneously. You will already have one. That is enough.

Take the floor early

Research on meeting dynamics shows that people who speak early are far more likely to contribute throughout. The longer you wait, the harder it becomes.

You do not need to say something significant. A short reaction, a relevant question, a brief comment on someone else’s point – any of these is enough to establish your presence. The goal is simple: get your point across before the agenda moves into more demanding territory, while the room is still warm and the stakes feel lower.

Buy yourself time

In any language, even fluent speakers lose the thread under pressure. In a second language, it happens more often – and the panic that follows makes it worse.

The solution is to have a small set of phrases ready that buy you time without drawing attention to the pause. “Let me think about that for a moment.” “That’s an interesting point – can I come back to it?” “If I understand correctly…” These are not signs of weakness. They signal that you take the conversation seriously – and they give you the seconds you need to find your words.

Read the room – and know when to hold back

Speaking up does not mean speaking constantly. Effective communicators read the room: they notice who has not spoken, who is struggling to get a word in, and when a topic needs more air rather than more words.

For managers, this is particularly important. When you hold back deliberately – to let a team member take the floor, to let an idea breathe – you are not losing ground. You are demonstrating the kind of presence that does not need to fill every silence.

The goal is not to talk more. It is to make what you say count.
