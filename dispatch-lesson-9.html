Straight Talk in the One-to-One
Straight Talk in the One-to-One

Anna had more work than usual this week, and she had noticed that requests for clarification from her team were becoming more and more frequent. One particular problem kept coming up: a team member had missed several internal deadlines, and Anna decided the issue needed to be addressed directly rather than left to grow.

She scheduled a one-to-one meeting for Thursday afternoon and spent some time preparing what she wanted to say. She remembered a difficult conversation from earlier in the year when she had got stuck halfway through an explanation and, for a moment, had been completely lost for words. This time she wanted to be ready, especially since it can be hard to stay clear and calm in the middle of a discussion about someone's performance.

The meeting began calmly, and her colleague explained the workload from his side. Partway through his explanation, Anna said, "Sorry for interrupting you, but I'd like to check something first: are we on the same page about what's been happening with the deadlines?" Her colleague nodded, and the two of them talked openly. Anna's comments were straightforward, and although she worried briefly that she might sound too direct, she avoided being blunt. She preferred to be frank rather than curt, and at one point she said, "To be quite frank, I think we need a different approach going forward."

After today's meeting, Anna felt the conversation had gone well. She agreed to check on progress at the next meeting, confident that being clear early on had prevented a much bigger problem later.

Building Bridges with Head Office
Building Bridges with Head Office

The finance team's next call with head office was about the outstanding invoices, and Anna knew the numbers needed to be right. The project could be at risk if the figures were unclear, so she wanted to make sure everyone on the call understood the current position before it started. As team leader, it was also her job to ensure that the new analyst prepared his section properly.

Anna cared about more than just accuracy, though. She had learned early in her career that you need to take care of your relationships with colleagues, especially with people you rarely see in person. She also knew that if problems are ignored for too long, relationships may sour, and she had no intention of letting that happen here. Instead, she tried to foster good relationships with the head office team through regular, informal check-ins, believing that it takes time to build relationships that survive difficult conversations. She had also seen what happened when a supplier cut ties with a client after months of ignored complaints, and she didn't want the same mistake repeated internally.

Head office etiquette was slightly different from what Anna was used to: shorter small talk, earlier starts, and a stronger focus on numbers. She had noticed that the two offices had a lot of traits in common, but the differences still mattered. On the call, Anna reported that the total outstanding balance had reached PLN 1 billion across the group; when a newer colleague looked confused by the group's larger reporting unit, she reminded him that one trillion is one thousand billion. Reading out the more detailed figures, she gave the error rate as nought point two three percent for the current month, then corrected the previous month's figure to nought point nought five percent.

By the end of the call, everyone understood the figures. The team had agreed on who would be held accountable for completing each action point and confirmed that every remaining task would be delivered on time.
